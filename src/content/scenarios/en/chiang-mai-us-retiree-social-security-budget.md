Probably yes for ordinary monthly spending. The harder parts are the visa paperwork, a serious illness and tax. **Plan on a Non-Immigrant O retirement visa, then one-year retirement extensions in Thailand. Keep Medicare Part B while a return to the US is still plausible. Buy proper Thai health cover instead of assuming low hospital prices make going uninsured safe.**

This plan is for **Linda, 66, single, a retired Ohio school administrator**. She receives **USD 2,350 a month in Social Security plus a USD 900 a month pension**, has **USD 140,000 in savings**, and wants to move in **October 2027**. Her projected age on arrival is 66 or 67, depending on her birthday. Everything here was checked on 7 November 2026. Rules, fees and prices must be rechecked before a 2027 move.

## The answer in one minute

**Affordable on this income.** USD 2,350 + USD 900 = USD 3,250 a month, or USD 39,000 a year. At an illustrative **35 THB per USD** that is **THB 113,750 a month**. The cautious monthly plan below costs **THB 73,948** (USD 2,112.80). That leaves **THB 39,802** (USD 1,137.20) before any tax and before the costs this plan has not priced.

**Not a “wealthy pensioner” case.** USD 39,000 is below even the USD 40,000 lower income threshold for the Long-Term Resident (LTR) wealthy pensioner programme. That lower route also needs a USD 250,000 qualifying Thai investment, which is more than all of Linda’s savings. The Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) is not a retirement category either, and Linda has no remote-work or other qualifying activity to show. [V6, V7]

**Why Non-O rather than O-A?** Both fit her age. Non-O has a shorter first stay. But it does not tie her residence to the O-A’s compulsory insurance certificate or its extra first-time police and medical paperwork. That is administrative flexibility, not advice to go without health cover. [V1–V3]

**Three things still open.** First, a newer Thai retirement order was found but its text could not be read, so it was not translated or checked. Second, hospital prices come from one hospital only. Third, no price for catastrophic care (intensive care, major surgery, cancer treatment) was found. Details are at the end.

A visa’s validity is the period in which you may use it. The stamp you receive on entry decides how long you may stay. Annual extensions are separate decisions, not an automatic permanent right. At 66, Linda is over the age-50 minimum. **Neither retirement route allows work.** [V1–V3]

## Make Non-O plus annual extension workable

**Apply from the US with the correct checklist.** Washington asks for a passport valid within 6 months of travel, a photograph and proof of your current location, alongside the financial evidence. Allow its published minimum of **30 working days** before travel. The O-A instead asks for 18 months of passport validity, FBI clearance, a medical certificate dated within 3 months and insurance, with a published lead time of 20 working days. Before any future application, check which consulate or e-Visa checklist covers Ohio. [V1, V2]

**Prefer the deposit route for the first extension, if the bank allows it.** Plan for **THB 800,000** in Linda’s own eligible Thai bank account at least 2 months before the extension application. Keep it there for 3 months after approval. After that, keep at least THB 400,000, and restore THB 800,000 at least 2 months before the next application. Use bank certification and account records. These timings come from an official summary last dated 2023, so revalidate them locally. **Opening a Thai account and getting the transfer seasoned within the first 90 days is a real execution risk.** [V3]

**Income route as a backup.** At 35 THB per USD, USD 3,250 is THB 113,750 a month, above THB 65,000. But earning enough is not the same as having acceptable evidence. Find out which pension and Social Security documents, and which Thai bank transfer history, the Chiang Mai office will accept for a first extension. **Do not assume an embassy income letter or one large transfer will do.** An annual income-plus-deposit combination is also listed, but Linda does not need that more complicated route. [V3]

**Keep the immigration money separate.** THB 800,000 is about USD 22,857.14. The THB 400,000 you must keep afterwards is about USD 11,428.57. Immigration funds are not emergency cash, and a health emergency does not excuse breaking the balance conditions.

**Ongoing administration:**

- **90-day reporting:** report your address after each 90 days of continuous stay. The published in-person window runs from 15 days before to 7 days after the due date. This does not renew your stay. Leaving and re-entering resets the count. Online reporting rules and windows were not verified, so use the local office’s current channel. [V4]
- **Annual extension:** apply before your permitted stay expires and keep the finances in place. Set separate reminders for the extension, the bank seasoning period, insurance and address reporting. [V5]
- **Re-entry permit:** get one before you leave while on an extension, or you lose the remaining stay. Dated official fees: THB 1,000 single, THB 3,800 multiple. A re-entry permit does not extend your expiry date. [V5]

Counterintuitive finding: the visa that looks lighter at the start can be the better long-term plan. A longer first O-A stay does not remove annual financial evidence or the insurance requirement.

## Keep a US landing pad: Medicare Part B

**Keep Part B during the trial move if returning is a realistic possibility.** Original Medicare generally does not pay for ordinary care in Chiang Mai, so keeping it buys continuity for care in the US, not local cover. The April 2026 Medicare fact sheet describes only narrow foreign-care exceptions. Medigap emergency travel benefits are not long-term expatriate cover. Medicare Advantage overseas benefits depend on the plan. [M2]

**Dropping Part B can cost you for life.** The usual surcharge is 10% for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not, unless an exception or Special Enrollment Period applies. Living abroad, or buying a Thai policy, should not be assumed to create a Special Enrollment Period. Returning is not an instant automatic reinstatement, so check enrollment timing and possible coverage gaps before cancelling. [M1, M3]

Dated 2026 illustration, not a 2027 quote: standard Part B is USD 202.90 a month, or USD 2,434.80 a year. After two full uncovered years with no exception, the surcharge is 20% × USD 202.90 = USD 40.58, making USD 243.48, which Medicare rounds to USD 243.50 a month. The real penalty would use the premium in force at that time. [M1]

**Two things about Linda are unknown.** First, whether her present Medicare enrollment is in place. Second, whether the USD 2,350 Social Security figure is gross or already net of Part B. The budget below treats it as gross and deducts Part B once. If it is already net, remove that deduction or restate the gross income. Review Medigap and any retiree plan rights before changing coverage; they have not been determined. Check how Social Security pays abroad through its public guidance before you leave; no individual screening was done. [S1]

## Bring these tax questions to an adviser

**Do not treat all retirement income as one tax bucket.** US citizens are generally taxed on worldwide income, subject to filing thresholds and relief. Moving does not itself end US returns. Thai tax residence is generally 180 or more days in a calendar year, under the Revenue Department’s newer explainer. An October 2027 arrival cannot reach 180 days by year-end on its own, but earlier 2027 visits to Thailand count, and a full 2028 is a different case. [T2, T3]

**Thai remittance rules changed in 2024.** The explainer says foreign income earned from 1 January 2024 while Thai tax-resident can be taxable when brought into Thailand, even in a later year. Income from before 2024, and income earned while not Thai tax-resident, is treated differently. Treaty rights can override domestic taxing rights, but reporting and proof still need advice. **Do not assume savings are exempt merely because they sit in an old bank account.** [T2]

Questions to put to a US–Thai tax adviser:

- **Social Security:** does Article 20(2) of the US–Thailand treaty make Linda’s US benefits taxable only in the US, with the Article 1(3)(a) saving-clause exception preserved? The text points that way. What evidence and Thai filing treatment are needed to claim it? “US-only” does not mean US-tax-free. [T1]
- **The USD 900 pension:** who legally pays it, and from what fund? If it is a government, state or local-authority pension for public service, does Article 21(2) give the US exclusive taxing rights? Linda is not stated to be a Thai national; ask whether any business-service exception applies. Do not assume a retired school administrator has a private pension. [T1]
- **If the pension is private:** how do Article 20(1)’s residence-state rule and the US-citizen saving clause in Article 1(2) work together? How do Article 25 credit relief and any re-sourcing work? US reporting does not stop just because the treaty points to residence-state taxation. [T1]
- **Savings tracing:** separate the USD 140,000 principal from interest, dividends or realized gains. Document when each amount arose and whether she was Thai-resident that year. How will mixed-account remittances, and transfers for the visa deposit, be evidenced? [T2]
- **First two tax years:** count all 2027 and 2028 Thai days. Confirm treaty residence, filing obligations, current remittance rules and any changes enacted by then. Which treaty-claim documents, tax-payment certificates, exchange rates and returns are required? [T1, T2]
- **US and Ohio housekeeping:** taxable Social Security and pension amounts, withholding, foreign tax credits, whether the foreign earned income exclusion has any relevance to retirement income, Ohio domicile and residency, and the FBAR and Form 8938 duties once she opens Thai accounts. No state-tax conclusion or final tax bill is given here. [T3]

**The spending cushion below is before US and Thai tax.** Neither a visa category nor a way of sending money abroad is a tax exemption. LTR tax benefits do not help someone who does not qualify for the LTR.

**Savings are a buffer, not income.** USD 140,000 × 35 = THB 4,900,000. After setting aside the THB 800,000 immigration deposit and the illustrative THB 500,000 health reserve, THB 3,600,000 remains (USD 102,857.14), before relocation costs, taxes or other commitments. The deposit is still Linda’s own asset, but treat it as restricted and do not count it twice as an emergency reserve.

**What the monthly plan leaves out:** a car, full-time help or long-term care, an uninsured major illness, Medigap, Part D or other US plan premiums, tax bills and large one-off purchases. Add your real obligations before deciding. Annual insurance premiums may be payable up front even though the budget spreads them monthly.

## What would make October 2027 ready

- **Now:** find out whether Social Security is gross or net, your current Medicare enrollment, who pays the pension and what type it is, your health conditions and prescription needs. Keep the option of returning to the US funded.
- **Before booking a permanent move:** recheck 2027 embassy and Chiang Mai rules, whether a Thai bank account is feasible and the seasoning timetable. Get personally underwritten insurance terms. Have a US–Thai tax adviser review the first two tax years.
- **On a trial stay:** inspect accessible condos and real transport routes. Test whether local care and daily life work for you. Keep a seasonal relocation reserve rather than committing all savings to property.
- **Only then:** choose the lease, transfer schedule, insurance and extension route.

**Bottom line:** Linda’s finances support a modest Chiang Mai retirement without drawing down savings for ordinary monthly spending. Health-insurance underwriting and future tax and immigration requirements are the deciding uncertainties. Non-O plus annual extensions is the best starting plan; the LTR does not fit, and the DTV is not justified by her facts.
