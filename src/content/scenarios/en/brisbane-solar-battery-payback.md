You want a smaller electricity bill, not an expensive system that only looks good beside a rebate countdown. For this northside Brisbane family of four, with a roughly $2,600 annual bill, **start with a sound 6.6kW solar design near $6,600 and defer the battery decision**. Try daytime timers and plan the electrical work for a future EV. The solar example saves $1,143 in its first full year, about $1,043 after maintenance. A $9,000 battery adds only $758/year at the assumed flat tariff: **11.9-year incremental simple payback before degradation**.

All amounts are AUD. Prices, policy and tariff evidence date from November 7, 2026 in Brisbane; they have not been refreshed for this guide. These are planning examples, not roof-specific quotes or guaranteed savings. Your actual tariff is unknown. **The three exact Nectr tariffs below are restricted existing-customer offers, not a verified shopping shortlist or confirmed sign-up options for your home.**

## What to budget—and what the headline price leaves out

Allow around $6,000–$8,000 for 6.6kW solar, $8,000–$11,200 for 10kW, and roughly $8,000–$10,500 for a mainstream 10–13kWh battery. Premium equipment and difficult sites can cost more. These ranges bring together different published examples; they are not precise Brisbane averages for every size. Where federal discounts are included, **do not subtract another rebate**.

### A smaller discount is not a reason to rush

The [Cheaper Home Batteries Program schedule](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-renewable-energy-systems/solar-batteries) continues through 2030. For the first 14kWh usable capacity, the STC factor is 6.8 in May–December 2026, 5.7 in January–June 2027 and 5.2 in July–December 2027. The next 14kWh gets 60% of that factor; the 28–50kWh band gets 15%. **Installation date, not signing date, determines support.**

At an assumed $39.90 per STC, a 12kWh usable battery illustrates the change:

| Installation period | Whole STCs, rounded down | Illustrative discount |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| May–December 2026 | 81 | About $3,232 |
| January–June 2027 | 68 | About $2,713 |
| July–December 2027 | 62 | About $2,474 |

Agent fees and traded STC prices change the dollars. This is not a guaranteed 30% cheque, and the model's net purchase prices already include the discount. The [solar PV deeming schedule](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-technology-certificates/calculate-small-scale-technology-certificate-entitlements) also falls from 5 years in 2026 to 4 in 2027 and 3 in 2028.

Battery eligibility includes new approved batteries of 5–100kWh nominal capacity, paired with solar, with eligible usable capacity up to 50kWh. Installation needs the relevant SAA battery endorsement. Grid-connected systems need VPP capability; capability alone does not mean you must join a virtual power plant. There is only one battery STC claim per premises, so future expansion can lose subsidy eligibility. Confirm the exact design before ordering.

For this owner-occupied home, the model counts $0 Queensland support. No open owner-occupier solar/battery rebate or state interest-free loan was verified. Old [Battery Booster material](https://www.energyandclimate.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0026/52964/retailer-fact-sheet-battery-booster.pdf) and the [historical state loan reference](https://www.parliament.qld.gov.au/Work-of-the-Assembly/Tabled-Papers/redirect/docs/2020/5720T301.pdf) do not establish current entitlement; their official closure documents were not successfully retrieved, so no exact closure dates are claimed. The [Supercharged Solar for Renters program](https://www.business.qld.gov.au/industries/mining-energy-water/energy/renewable/supercharged-solar-for-renters), opened December 12, 2025, offers up to $3,500 for eligible landlords with rented-out properties—not this owner-occupied home.

Standard time of use: peak 4pm–8:59pm, off-peak 11am–3:59pm, shoulder 9pm–10:59am. Solar Sharer: peak 4pm–8:59pm, free imports 11am–1:59pm, 8.02c from 2pm–3:59pm and 26.15c from 9pm–10:59am. These bands apply all week, all year. Solar Sharer's timezone remains unconfirmed here; ask the retailer how your meter applies the periods.

A high feed-in advertisement does not automatically mean a cheap bill. Check export caps, usage rates, supply charges and eligibility together. No 44c legacy solar-bonus entitlement is assumed here.

### The flat-rate model is a calibration, not an offer

Annual use is 8,030kWh, or 22kWh/day. At assumed 27c imports and $1.18/day supply, `8,030 × $0.27 + 365 × $1.18 = $2,598.80/year`, close to the stated $2,600. The assumed feed-in tariff is 4c/kWh. **None of these three inputs is a verified retailer offer.** At Nectr's published single-rate charges, the same no-solar use costs $2,917.37/year with zero retailer feed-in—not your current bill.

### Time of use can help storage—but compare final bills

The separate workbook sensitivity uses assumed 45c peak, 28c shoulder and 20c off-peak rates, with the same supply charge and 4c feed-in. These are **not any Nectr offer**. Household use is split 40% peak, 35% shoulder and 25% off-peak. Direct solar displaces 10% peak, 60% shoulder and 30% off-peak; battery discharge displaces 80% peak and 20% shoulder.

That gives a $3,065 no-solar bill, $1,153 solar-only bill savings and $2,402 combined savings. Final bills are about $1,911 solar-only and $663 combined, before $100 maintenance. Battery incremental savings rise to $1,248/year, or roughly 7.2-year simple payback at $9,000. The no-solar baseline is also higher. A larger saving alone does not prove the tariff is better.

**Counterintuitive finding:** free midday imports can make your own midday solar less valuable. Under Solar Sharer, solar replacing a free 11am–2pm import avoids no usage charge in that window. A battery might still avoid evening peak prices, but a flat-rate model cannot establish that result. Compare your interval use, solar profile and actual eligible plan before switching.

## What the solar and battery examples deliver

The [Government Solar Consumer Guide](https://www.energy.gov.au/solar/get-know-solar-technology/solar-panels) gives Brisbane an annual-average yield of 4.2kWh per kW per day. That means about 10,118kWh/year from 6.6kW or 15,330kWh/year from 10kW. This is representative yield, not a prediction for your roof.

Assume daylight solar directly supplies 40% of the 8,030kWh household load: 3,212kWh. That is **31.7% of 6.6kW generation**, not 40% solar self-consumption. A 12kWh usable battery delivers 3,360kWh/year at 280 equivalent cycles. At 90% round-trip efficiency, it needs 3,733kWh of surplus solar. Solar self-use, including charging losses, rises to 68.6%; useful household load served rises from 40% to 81.8%. Those percentages measure different things.

| Option | Net purchase cost | Annual bill reduction | Remaining annual bill | Simple payback, years | 10-year net savings |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 6.6kW solar | $6,600 | $1,143 | $1,455 | 6.3 | $3,745 |
| 10kW solar | $9,350 | $1,352 | $1,247 | 7.5 | $3,033 |
| 6.6kW plus battery | $15,600 | $1,901 | $697 | 8.7 | $1,677 |
| 10kW plus battery | $18,350 | $2,110 | $489 | 9.1 | $965 |
| Wait 2 years, then 6.6kW solar | $6,600 | $1,143 | $1,455 | 6.3 after installation | $1,692 |

Annual figures are the first full operating year, without an EV. Bill reduction and remaining bill exclude maintenance. Simple payback is purchase cost divided by first-year bill reduction less $100 annual maintenance; it is not discounted or the actual degradation-adjusted break-even date. Waiting adds 2 years to elapsed payback from the start.

Ten-year net means cumulative bill savings less maintenance and purchase cost over the same horizon starting November 7, 2026. It assumes 0.5% annual PV degradation and 2% annual battery-throughput degradation, with constant nominal tariffs and costs. There is no finance, electricity inflation, replacement or terminal/resale value. A $2,000 repair reduces net savings by $2,000. Bill credits are allowed, not silently capped at zero.

Battery charging is solar-only: no VPP revenue or grid-arbitrage income. Supply charges remain payable. Annual matching is approximate, not an interval simulation. Export clipping, seasonal mismatch, reserve settings, standby use, outages and power limits are not simulated. Reduce yield, cycles or direct-load share when testing uncertainty rather than treating these savings as guaranteed.

### Judge the battery on its own extra return

The battery adds $9,000 and initially saves $758/year beyond solar alone: `3,360 × 27c − (3,360 ÷ 0.9) × 4c`. Its 11.9-year incremental simple payback is before degradation. Over ten years it contributes about $6,932 of bill reductions, less than its purchase cost.

Backup resilience may still matter to your family. It is not counted as cash savings. Ask separately about backed-up circuits, reserve settings and inverter power limits; owning a battery does not establish the backup design you need.

### Waiting needs a real reason, not a price prediction

The wait example pays ordinary bills for 2 years, installs November 7, 2028 and gets 8 operating years inside the same horizon. It assumes the **same net installed price** in 2028—not the same rebate and not a forecast. The workbook's future-price multiplier must cover market changes and smaller STCs together.

Waiting cuts 6.6kW ten-year net savings by about $2,053. Its future net price needs to fall about 31%, to roughly $4,547, just to match installing at the starting date under these assumptions. Roof repairs or household cash needs can still justify waiting.

## An EV in 2028 helps most when it is home in daylight

The EV sensitivity assumes 12,000km/year, 0.18kWh/km and 90% charging efficiency: 2,400kWh/year at home, starting projection year 3 on November 7, 2028. There is no partial-year EV adjustment. With 60% daytime direct charging, 1,440kWh replaces grid imports. At 27c imports less 4c forgone exports, solar gains another $331/year. No petrol savings or charger cost is credited to solar.

| Option | 10-year net without EV | 10-year net with EV from 2028 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 6.6kW solar | $3,745 | $6,394 |
| 10kW solar | $3,033 | $5,683 |
| 6.6kW plus battery | $1,677 | $4,326 |
| 10kW plus battery | $965 | $3,615 |

At the flat-rate assumption, the EV adds $648/year to the no-solar bill. Daytime solar reduces that extra cost to about $317/year; overnight-only charging adds little solar-only benefit. Each comparison includes the same EV load with and without solar.

In this annual model, 6.6kW already has enough surplus for the EV. The extra 3.4kW costs $2,750 but earns only about $2,039 of extra exports over ten years. Consider 10kW if the incremental quote is around $2,000 or less, daytime demand is higher, or a roof- and interval-specific model supports it—not simply because an EV is coming.

### Try a less sunny financial case

At zero feed-in tariff, 6.6kW gross savings fall to $867/year and payback rises to 8.6 years after maintenance. If only 25% of household load is directly solar-served, payback is about 8.6 years at 4c feed-in. At 55%, it is about 5.0 years. Daytime habits and your actual contract can matter more than a larger panel count.

## Before you pay a deposit

- **Check the roof first.** Look at remaining roof life, shade, usable area, orientation and seasonal sun. North generally helps annual yield; east/west can spread generation across your day. Ask for shading-adjusted annual and monthly generation, including inverter clipping.
- **Look at your interval data.** Hybrid work does not guarantee sunny weekdays at home. Check air-con peaks, hot-water controls, daytime loads and EV parking. Try timers before assuming storage is necessary.
- **Check the person and products separately.** Installer/design accreditation is SAA, with the relevant PV or battery endorsement; “CEC-accredited installer” is legacy wording. Check Queensland electrical licences, CEC-approved panels/inverter/battery and New Energy Tech Approved Seller status separately. None replaces the others.
- **Read the warranties.** The [consumer warranty guide](https://www.energy.gov.au/solar/get-know-solar-technology/warranties-and-insurance) distinguishes product and performance cover. Compare inverter cover, workmanship, local labour/travel costs and Australian support. Battery cover may stop at years, cycles or energy throughput; check retained capacity, reserve rules and VPP exclusions. A 25-year panel performance warranty is not a 25-year whole-system warranty.
- **Get the connection offer before committing.** Under the [Energex guidance](https://www.energex.com.au/our-services/connections/residential-and-commercial-connections/solar-connections-and-other-technologies/dynamic-connections-for-energy-exports/dynamic-connections-for-installers), normal basic export is 5kW per phase, but restricted or zero-export outcomes can apply. Dynamic export can reach 10kW per phase, not continuously guaranteed. The cited single-phase hybrid inverter cap is 10kVA. Panel kW, inverter kVA, export kW and battery kWh are different quantities. Ask for your approved envelope, communications, fallback rules and costs.
- **Compare complete scope.** Get equipment model numbers, usable battery capacity, inverter power, switchboard/meter work, roof extras, monitoring, export equipment, an EV circuit and backup circuits in writing. “Battery-ready wiring” alone does not prove hybrid-inverter compatibility.
- **Leave pressure deadlines outside the decision.** Get three written, site-specific comparisons. Read deposit, cancellation, finance-versus-cash price, workmanship and service terms. A “free battery” or no-bill promise needs explicit assumptions.

Use the editable solar and battery workbook (original supporting file is not published; use the information in this guide) to replace the blue Inputs cells with your bill, eligible offer and complete quotes. Read its Read me sheet; Summary, Annual and TOU separate the main results from assumptions. Workbook changes do not update this guide. A practical next step is a 6.6kW design, a separately priced 10kW alternative and summer/winter monitoring before revisiting storage.

## FAQ

### Should we buy the battery before the discount drops?

Not on the discount alone. The net battery budget already includes federal support, yet its extra flat-rate return is weak. Better interval data, a sharper quote, a suitable TOU/VPP contract or the personal value of backup could change the decision.

### Can we sign up for one of these three Nectr plans?

That is not confirmed. All three are restricted existing-customer offers, with additional moving/carry-over or smart-meter conditions. Confirm your address, meter and network tariff with the retailer; this is not a new-customer shopping shortlist.

### Does the future EV mean we need 10kW or a battery?

Not automatically. Daytime charging improves direct solar use, and 6.6kW has enough annual surplus in this example. Your parking hours, seasonal output and power limits still need a closer check.
